The FTC Wants to See the Receipts on AI Safety

By The Bunker Scale · October 15, 2026

Rating as of October 15, 2026: 5/10 · Eyebrow Raised

After months of AI agent incidents, a federal regulator isn't just asking the labs to promise they're being careful. It is asking for the paperwork.

What changed

Axios reported that the Federal Trade Commission has sent civil investigative demands, which work like subpoenas, to OpenAI and Anthropic [1]. Reuters and others followed, citing Axios's reporting [2][3].

According to the reports [1][2][3]:

  • The demands seek documents about AI safety and security incidents, the companies' safeguards, the claims they make about their products, and their privacy practices.
  • They reportedly run more than 50 pages each.
  • The FTC also plans to compel executives from Anthropic, OpenAI and the research group METR to testify.
  • Reuters had reported last month that the FTC was planning an industry-wide investigation into AI companies.

The FTC's job includes policing "unfair or deceptive" business practices. The question here seems to be whether safety failures, or claims about safety, could cross that line under existing consumer protection law [1].

OpenAI said it "will engage constructively with the FTC" [2].

Why it matters

Until recently, most of the response to the AI agent incidents has been voluntary: company blog posts, voluntary reporting, voluntary pledges. A White House statement last week said reporting incidents is mandatory, but didn't say how it would be enforced.

This is different in kind. Civil investigative demands are legally compulsory. If the FTC eventually decides that AI companies can be held responsible under existing law when their agents cause harm, the companies would have a much more concrete reason than reputation to keep those agents contained.

What you can probably ignore

"The FTC is charging OpenAI and Anthropic." An investigative demand gathers evidence. No violation has been found, and no fine or restriction has been issued.

"This will stop AI development." Nothing about this restricts what the companies can build.

Why it earned this rating

On the side of a higher number:

  • It is a compulsory federal investigation focused squarely on safety incidents [1].
  • It could eventually create legal liability for AI agent harms.

On the side of a lower number:

  • It's early. Investigations can take a long time and may end with nothing.
  • It doesn't change any AI system's capabilities or behavior.
  • The details so far come from anonymous sources [2].

Concrete government action that is still at the evidence-gathering stage is a 5.

What I'm watching next

  • Whether more AI companies receive demands.
  • Executive testimony, if it happens and becomes public.
  • Any finding or settlement, which would be the real turning point.
